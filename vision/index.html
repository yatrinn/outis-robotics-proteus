Outis / Our vision

Intelligence should be able to choose its body.

Smaller modules. More possible machines.
A future in which the robot’s body adapts to the work.

Yannik Trinn & Ruben Estrella · 24 October 2026 · 4 min read

The idea

Many useful bodies Smaller modules Embodied AI The first proof

A robot’s body determines where it can go, what it can reach and how much force it can apply. Better software can expand its abilities. The geometry of the machine still shapes the work it can do.

We are building Proteus to make that geometry a choice.

Our long-term vision is a supply of robotic modules that can connect, move and reorganize into useful machines. Give the system a task. It should work out the structure it needs, assemble it, do the work and adapt when conditions change. The body becomes another part of the problem the intelligence can solve.

One system. Many useful bodies.

Imagine sending a tool into a damaged machine room. The modules enter in a slender configuration, establish support and form a working structure around the tool. On the next job, the same inventory becomes a carrier, a fixture or a different manipulator.

Further ahead, a workshop could keep a pool of modules that changes with its workload. Smaller, more affordable modules could bring that capability into laboratories, farms and eventually homes. People would ask for work to be done; the system would choose its body and the tools it needs.

That is the scale of our ambition: broadly available physical capability, adaptable to many tasks. Each new task would draw on a growing library of configurations, tools and skills. Each successful deployment would make the same platform more useful.

Think smaller. Build more.

We intend to reduce module size substantially across successive generations. More, smaller building blocks could fit through narrower openings, form finer structures and distribute support and movement in new ways.

Miniaturization needs its own engineering breakthroughs. Actuators, docking mechanisms, power delivery and manufacturing have to work together at each scale. More connections also demand better reliability and recovery. A useful smaller module must still deliver force and survive repeated operation.

We will choose sizes around the capabilities they unlock. There may be several module families: work inside machinery, carrying equipment across damaged terrain and everyday manipulation have different physical requirements. We want each generation to expand what the system can do and reduce the cost of doing it.

Embodied AI with a body it can change.

A command such as “place this sensor behind that opening” contains several problems: understand the scene, select a tool, choose a structure, find a feasible way to assemble it and control the resulting robot.

We want to combine advances in perception and learned planning with explicit models of geometry, connections, loads and energy. The system must reason about how it becomes a useful machine, as well as how that machine moves. A transformation is part of the task.

Real deployments would generate experience about which configurations work, where transitions fail and how to recover. Our aim is to improve the hardware and the planner together, then transfer useful skills across configurations. The long-term opportunity is a platform whose physical abilities grow with its software, tools and module inventory.

The first proof is physical.

Our first prototype moves and performs simple motorized docking on a test rig. We are developing simulation and planning tools alongside it. Full autonomous reconfiguration into working multi-module machines is a capability we are working toward.

We start with industrial tasks where access and useful force matter: reaching into equipment to retrieve objects, inspect or place sensors. These jobs give us concrete conditions in which to measure performance and learn what customers need.

A task should be able to call forth the machine it needs. We are building toward that future, one working module at a time.
