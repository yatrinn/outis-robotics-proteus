The problem

Someone still has to go in first.

Process vessels, columns, voids in collapsed buildings: today the work behind a small opening means opening the equipment or a confined-space entry with permits and a standby attendant. Tools slim enough to fit cannot apply force at reach; robots strong enough to work do not fit.

Send a robot. Lose a module, not a person.

How it works

Every fixed-shape robot fails one phase of the job.

A fixed robot is built for the hardest moment of its mission and carries that shape everywhere. Proteus changes shape per phase: it walks, slips through as a chain, braces and works with force, then walks back.

1Walker

Walks where wheels stop.

Crosses rubble, steps and gaps with the sensor or tool on board. Nobody has to cross first.

2Chain

Fits through 25 cm.

Turns into a chain and reaches through manways, nozzles and gaps in collapsed slabs.

3Braced arm

Works with force, on site.

Braces against the structure and presses tools and sensors on with a force a snake or a drone cannot apply.

The module

Everything is made of one part.

One actuated strut with a docking head at each end. Identical struts dock into the shape the job needs, share power through their docks and change shape under load. The module and its docks are IP-protected.

Applications

Work behind the opening, in plants and in the field.

Proteus retrieves, inspects and places sensors or tools where access is small and entry is dangerous, so nobody has to go in first.

Energy and industrial plants

Vessels, columns and exchangers: retrieve, inspect and place sensors through nozzles and manways. No confined-space entry, fewer openings, less downtime per intervention.

Defense maintenance and protection

Aircraft fuel tanks and vehicle hulls, ordnance disposal and CBRN reconnaissance: the modules go into the hazard, soldiers and technicians stay out.

Civil protection and rescue

Inspect collapsed structures and voids before anyone enters. The first look costs a module, not a rescuer.

Our vision

AI should be able to choose its own body.

Proteus redefines embodied AI.

Computers became universal when one machine could run any program. Robots have not had that moment yet. Each one is still built around a single body, and that body decides where it can go, what it can reach and how hard it can push. Robot brains get better every month. The body has become the bottleneck.

Proteus makes the body a choice. A pool of identical modules becomes the machine a task needs, splits into a team when there are several tasks and merges back when the job needs strength. One part, made by the million, does the work of a fleet of specialized machines: a broken module is swapped, not the machine, and capacity grows the way compute does.

Modular robots have spent decades in labs, built to prove that reconfiguration works. Ours is built for paid work from the first module on, with load-bearing docks and our own swarm intelligence that decides which body to become and moves the modules as one. Each generation gets smaller and opens new work.
